Interaction patterns

Decisions that recur across the app, each one arrived at by getting it wrong first. Worth reading before adding a control, so the next one behaves like the last.

Destructive actions

Two clicks, and the second one expires

Delete arms on the first click and confirms on the second, reverting after five seconds. A button left armed on a worktop is a trap: you come back, click the label you remember, and the recipe is gone.

→ armed →

Not window.confirm(). It is suppressed in some embedded contexts, which turns a destructive action into a silent no-op, and it cannot name the thing it is about to remove.

AI-assisted editing

It proposes, the reader decides

Anything a model suggests is shown beside what the recipe says now, with a reason, and applies only when accepted. Two buttons: Use this and Keep mine. Never a single "Fix" — that is the app deciding.

2 c. → 2 t.

The import is deliberately literal for the same reason: it copies "2 c. baking soda" exactly as the card has it. Correcting silently rewrites someone's own recipe and they never find out. A wrong number you can see beats a plausible one you cannot check.

ReviewRecipe · StylePicker

Generated values are validated, not trusted

Colours must be hex, faces must be keys the app actually loads, sizes are clamped, and anything containing braces, semicolons or url() is dropped — these values are written into a style attribute. A proposal that changes nothing, or arrives without a reason, is discarded rather than shown.

Saving

State is text; buttons are verbs

The save button always reads Save and disables when there is nothing to save. It used to read "Saved" — a state pretending to be an action, inviting a click that does nothing.

RecipeEditor save bar

Leaving saves; it doesn't ask

beforeunload does not fire on client-side navigation, so in-app links used to discard unsaved work in silence. Clicking one now saves first, and stays put if the save fails rather than navigating away from an error.

A confirm dialog was the obvious alternative and is worse: it puts the decision back on someone who has just expressed it by editing, and browsers can suppress it — which either traps them on the page or loses the work anyway.

Decisions already made are saved immediately: accepting a suggestion, applying a style, storing a picture. A half-typed recipe is a draft; a clicked "Use this" is not.

Controls that earn their space

Revealed by focus, never by hover alone

Per-step picture buttons appear when the step has focus, or when it already has a picture. Focus rather than hover so it works on a phone: tapping into the step is what reveals them. Hover-only controls do not exist on a touch screen.

The measure that justified it: three stacked buttons beside a one-line textarea made every step 72px instead of 43px — 145px of page over five steps, for controls nobody was using yet.

RecipeEditor method steps

Advanced controls start closed

Two menus, two sliders and six colour wells are a lot of permanently open controls for something most people set once, if ever. Presets answer it for nearly everyone; the manual panel is a <details>.

Where things live

Editing is the page; finishing is below it

Checking and appearance sit under the recipe, past a divider marked Finishing. They used to sit between the title and the ingredients, where three panels separated someone from the thing they opened the editor to change.

The cook view is the mirror image: it carries no controls for changing content at all. Input boxes are the wrong thing to have open on a worktop, and they print as empty rectangles.

RecipeEditor · cook page · share page

Paid features degrade, they don't error

A control that cannot work is not offered. With no Stripe keys the pricing page still renders and the buy button reads "Not available yet" — per plan, not per account, so a half-configured deployment sells the plan that is ready and hides the one that isn't.

Turning something off is never gated. Sharing is a paid feature; withdrawing a public link is not, because someone whose subscription lapsed must still be able to take it down.

plans.ts · canBuyPlan · share route

These are all the same rule seen from different sides: the person using this owns their recipes. The app can suggest, validate and warn — it does not decide, correct or discard on their behalf.
